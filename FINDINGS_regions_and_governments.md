# Findings: regions, standout countries and the coalition hypothesis

Analysis notes from 18–19 October 2026, written alongside the main [FINDINGS.md](FINDINGS.md). All numbers use the current index, which weights laws, budgets and stability equally (a third each). They cover 1950–2025; 2026 is excluded as incomplete. A region's score is the average of its member countries' averages. The laws pillar compares each country with its own history, so laws levels shouldn't be compared across countries.

**West, East and the Big five over time**

| Years | Western 20 | Eastern Europe | Big five |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1993–99 | 78.0 | 56.6 | 77.8 |
| 2000–09 | 78.3 | 79.2 | 73.9 |
| 2016–25 | 76.2 | 73.9 | 62.6 |

- **Western Europe as a whole barely moved.** Its steady small and Nordic states offset the Big five and Southern Europe.
- **Eastern Europe** closed a 21-point gap in the 2000s and moved slightly ahead of the West. It has fallen back since, mainly because of Bulgaria (down 31 points) and Romania (down 20).
- **The Big five** matched the Western average in the 1990s. They are now 14 points below it and about 11 below Eastern Europe. Their 2025 average of 47.3 is the lowest of any region.
- **What drove the Big five's fall,** comparing 2000–09 with 2016–25:
  - changes of prime minister went from 9 to 19;
  - late budgets went from 10% to 27.5% of years;
  - caretaker time went from 2.8% to 7.5% of the year.
- **Southern Europe's fall is mostly budgets.** Late budgets went from 3% to 22.5% of years, driven by Spain and Portugal. Greece rose about 10 points over the same period.

## 2. Standout countries, 2016–25

- **Bulgaria:** six early elections, the most in Europe, including the repeat elections of 2021–24. Its index fell from 90 to 60.
- **Romania:** nine changes of prime minister in ten years, the most of any country.
- **Netherlands:** 1,096 days under caretaker governments, the most in Europe, ahead of Spain's 688. It still scores 69 because its budgets pass on time.
- **Austria:** seven chancellors in ten years, yet up 11 points, because it now passes more laws than it used to.
- **Poland:** started every fiscal year from 2016 to 2025 without a budget law in force. Its budget is usually adopted in January or February.
- **Iceland:** the Nordic outlier, with three early elections, five prime ministers and a 15-point fall.
- **Greece:** up from 75 to 84 after the crisis years, with stable governments and on-time budgets.
- **Croatia:** up about 12 points, mainly because the late budgets of the 2000s stopped.

**Volatility.** This is the typical swing of the yearly index around a country's own average, 1995–2025.

Switzerland is steady partly by design: it has no prime minister, confidence votes or snap elections. Norway is next at 8.7. France is also among the steadiest, despite seven prime ministers in 2016–25, because its budgets almost always pass on time.

**The gap between best and worst is widening**

| Years | Best | Worst | Gap |

**Caveat on laws.** Malta, Finland and Lithuania gain 50–63 points on the laws pillar between 2000–09 and 2016–25. That only means they pass more laws than they used to. Their law records also start around 1990, which exaggerates the effect.

## 3. Italy's budgets since 1989

Italy has had its budget in force on 1 January every year since 1989; each year is backed by the Official Gazette law. Its last provisional budget covered January to March 1988.

- **Procedure reform.** Law 362 of 1988 created a fixed budget season in parliament and limited what the annual finance bill could contain.
- **End of secret ballots.** The Chamber of Deputies abolished secret votes on most bills in October 1988. Before that, government backbenchers routinely sank their own government's bills anonymously.
- **Euro pressure.** The Maastricht deficit targets in the 1990s made missing the budget deadline a market risk.

Italy now hits the deadline mainly through confidence votes on one giant amendment, often by 29 or 30 December, and the second chamber barely debates the bill. The budget pillar only checks the date, so it scores 100 without capturing how compressed parliament's role is.

## 4. Testing the hypothesis: are coalitions and lost majorities behind the recent falls?

The hypothesis: coalitions are becoming more common and absolute majorities rarer, which makes policymaking slower and more fragile. It was offered as the reason Germany, the UK, Spain and France fell after 2021.

**Method.** Each country-year gets the type of government that held office for most of that year: single-party majority, majority coalition, minority, or caretaker. The source is the REPDEM government records (with ParlGov for Switzerland, Cyprus and Malta).

**Are coalitions becoming more common? Only slightly.**

| Western 20 | 1950s | 1990s | 2010s | 2020s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Majority coalitions | 56% | 44% | 56% | 45% |
| Minority governments | 16% | 30% | 22% | 28% |
| Single-party majorities | 19% | 12% | 5% | 12% |
| Average parties in government | 2.6 | 3.1 | 3.1 | 3.1 |

Coalitions have been the norm since the 1950s. The shift is fewer single-party majorities and more minority governments. The average number of parties in government rose until the 1990s, then stopped.

**Do coalitions and minority governments score worse? Barely.** Each country is compared with its own average.

| Government type | Index vs own average | Budget pillar vs own average |
|---|---|---|
| Single-party majority | +0.9 | +5.7 |
| Majority coalition | +0.4 | −0.1 |
| Minority | −0.6 | −0.8 |
| Caretaker (no government formed) | −39.8 | −30.1 |

The number of coalition partners makes no difference either: two-, three- and four-party majorities all score within about a point of normal. Minority government works well where it is routine, such as the Nordics.

**The four countries, 2010–20 against 2021–25**

| Country | Government since 2021 | Index | Main driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | Three-party coalition, then two-party | 76 → 56 | Late budgets in 2022, 2024 and 2025; laws collapsed in 2023–25 |
| France | Majority until June 2022, then minority | 75 → 65 | Lost its majority; five prime ministers; stopgap special law for 2025 |
| Spain | Minority throughout | 58 → 61 | Strong 2021–22; no budget and very few laws in 2024–25 |
| UK | Single-party majority throughout | 61 → 59 | Prime-minister churn within one party; very low law output |

**Verdict.** "More coalitions" is the wrong mechanism. A sharper version fits: governments without a dependable majority for their budget do worse, whether that's a fractured coalition or a minority with unreliable partners. That explains France, Germany and Spain, but not the UK, whose decline is long-running and internal.

## 5. Germany, France and Spain, 2021–25

### Germany: a three-party coalition broke over money

| | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Index | 85 | 63 | 74 | 35 | 22 |
| Laws | 86 | 89 | 21 | 10 | 10 |
| Budget | 100 | 0 | 100 | 0 | 0 |
| Stability | 70 | 100 | 100 | 96 | 56 |

- **December 2021:** Scholz formed the coalition of SPD, Greens and FDP. The 2022 budget passed in June 2022, which is the normal lag after a German election; 2018 was similar.
- **November 2023:** the Constitutional Court blocked moving €60bn of unused COVID borrowing into the climate fund. The 2024 budget slipped to February 2024.
- **November 2024:** the coalition collapsed over the 2025 budget when Scholz fired his FDP finance minister. Scholz lost a confidence vote in December, and an early election followed on 23 February 2025.
- **2025:** Germany ran on provisional spending for about nine months. Merz's CDU/CSU–SPD government took office in May and passed the 2025 budget in September. The 2026 budget was on time.
- **Laws:** 75–87 a year in 2023–25, against about 100 a year in 2010–20. Germany also passed 183 laws in 2021, its busiest year on record, which makes the following years look weak.
- **Data note:** the 2025 election is recorded as a government defeat, not an early election, so it is penalised through the caretaker months and the change of chancellor.

### France: losing the majority

| | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Index | 76 | 77 | 71 | 63 | 39 |
| Laws | 28 | 45 | 12 | 43 | 36 |
| Budget | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 | 0 |
| Stability | 100 | 86 | 100 | 46 | 81 |

- **June 2022:** Macron's camp lost its absolute majority. Every government since has been a minority government.
- **2023–24:** budgets and the pension reform passed through Article 49.3, which passes bills without a vote. The index still scores those budgets as on time.
- **2024:** parliament was dissolved in June and the snap election left three blocs. Attal stayed on as caretaker for about two months. Barnier's five-party minority government was censured in December.
- **2025:** France opened the year on a stopgap "special law". The budget passed in February under Bayrou. Bayrou lost a confidence vote in September, and Lecornu's first government lasted less than a day.
- **2026:** another special law in December 2025, with the budget adopted in February 2026. This will show in the 2026 score.
- **Main drivers:** stability and budgets. Law output barely changed.

### Spain: a minority government that ran out of partners

| | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Index | 90 | 84 | 64 | 34 | 35 |
| Laws | 71 | 52 | 22 | 2 | 4 |
| Budget | 100 | 100 | 100 | 0 | 0 |
| Stability | 100 | 100 | 68 | 100 | 100 |

- **2021–22:** the PSOE–Unidas Podemos minority government relied on ERC, Bildu and the PNV for votes. It passed three budgets on time, the first since 2018, and 64–73 laws a year.
- **2023:** after losses in the May local elections, Sánchez called a general election for July, about five months early. A caretaker government followed from July to November.
- **2024–25:** the PSOE–Sumar minority government was invested with the votes of Junts in return for the amnesty law. It presented no budget for 2024 or 2025, so the 2023 budget was rolled over. Laws fell to 28–29 a year, and in 2024–25 roughly half of them were government decrees.
- **Junts:** it voted down several government measures, including an omnibus decree in January 2025. Its reported break with the government in October 2025 is not in the data and needs checking before it is quoted.
- **2026:** the source note in the budget data says no 2026 budget was adopted and that the Cortes were dissolved for an election on 29 November 2026. That would make three years running on the 2023 budget.
- **Main drivers:** laws and budgets. Stability stays at 100 because the government's survival isn't in doubt; it just can't legislate.

**The common thread.** All three fell when the government lost a reliable majority for its budget. Germany's coalition split, France lost its majority, and Spain's partners became unreliable.
