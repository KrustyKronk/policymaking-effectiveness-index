    What it measures

    The index asks a plain question: in a given year, did the government get its basic job done? It looks at three things you can count. How many substantive laws parliament passed, whether the budget was in force when the fiscal year began, and whether there was a government with full powers in office.

    Each pillar is scored 0 to 100 and the index is their weighted average. It started as a test of a hunch about Spain and the United Kingdom: that snap elections, caretaker spells and late budgets crowd out legislating. It now covers 31 EU and EFTA democracies, back to 1950 where the records allow.

    It does not judge whether laws are good, popular or well implemented. A government that passes many bad laws scores well on the first pillar. It is a measure of capacity to act, not of results.

    The three pillars

    The index is the weighted mean of the pillars that have data that year. Weights are rescaled over those pillars, so a missing pillar never counts as a zero. When law data is missing for a year that has other data, laws count as a neutral 50.

    The three pillars carry equal weight. An earlier version weighted laws 40, budgets 20 and stability 30. Its rankings were almost the same: Spain was last for 2016–25 either way.

    Years before a country's democratic start (Spain before 1979, the post-communist states before their first free parliaments) are not scored at all.

    Who is covered when

    Records start at different points. Ten long-standing democracies go back to 1950 (panel A). The Western 20 are complete from about 1980 (panel B). All 31 are in from the early 1990s (panel C). The averages on the Explore page only include countries with data in each year, so the early decades describe fewer countries.

    Scoring eras

    The laws pillar compares each country with its own history, not with its neighbours. Parliaments differ hugely in how they package legislation: Italy passes hundreds of small laws a year, the UK a few dozen long ones. A raw cross-country count would only measure drafting style. So a year scores high on laws when that parliament passed more than it usually does.

    Where a country changed regime, its history restarts, so it is only compared with itself inside one system:

    CountryErasWhy
    Italy1948–1992 · 1993–Electoral reform and the collapse of the First Republic party system
    Germany1949–1989 · 1990–Reunification
    France1959–Fifth Republic
    Spain, Portugal, Greece1979–, 1976–, 1975–Transitions to democracy
    Central and Eastern Europe1990–1993 onwardFirst free parliaments; Croatia from 2000

    Budgets and stability are on the same scale everywhere, so those two pillars do compare countries directly.

    Budget calendars

    The budget test is whether spending authority was in force on the first day of the fiscal year. Most countries start on 1 January, but not all, and some changed:

    CountryFiscal year startsNote
    Italy1 July until 1964, then 1 JanuaryProvisional budgets (esercizio provvisorio) were routine until 1988
    Germany1 April until 1960, then 1 JanuaryProvisional budget management in most post-election years
    Denmark1 April until 1977/78, then 1 January
    Sweden1 July until 1995/96, then 1 January1995/96 ran for 18 months
    United Kingdom1 AprilNot scored: spending is authorised by rolling Supply and Appropriation Acts, so there is no single on-time test
    Ireland1 JanuaryNot scored for the same reason: estimates are voted during the year
    Netherlands1 JanuaryOn time means the lower house voted before 1 January; the Senate often finishes in spring

    A budget in force on day one scores 100. One adopted within the grace period (30 days by default) scores 50, or 25 when the delay is known to be late but not by how much. Anything later, a rolled-over budget or provisional twelfths, scores 0.

    Caveats

    • Fewer but longer laws. Over 50 to 75 years the UK, France and Italy moved towards fewer, longer statutes. Part of their long decline in the laws pillar is drafting style, not inactivity. Budgets and stability are unaffected.
    • Switzerland has a consensus executive with no prime minister, confidence votes or snap elections. Its stability score is close to 100 by construction.
    • France joins three law sources: Senate yearly statistics from 1959, Comparative Agendas Project records 1979–2001, and the official DOLE dossiers from 2002.
    • Germany 1949–1972 law counts are per-parliament totals spread evenly across years.
    • Italy in the 1950s: the Camera record holds about 45% of all laws by number. It is consistent within the era, which is what the laws pillar compares.
    • Cyprus before 1992 numbered every law in one series. Appropriation, guarantee and treaty laws are excluded by title.
    • Short law series. Sweden from 2003, Bulgaria from 2009 (counts from 2003), Latvia from 2010 (counts from 2000), Finland from 1990, Romania from 1996. Earlier years use budgets and stability only, with laws at a neutral 50.
    • Unknown budget years. Denmark and Finland before 1980, Belgium 1980–87, Norway 1980–86 and Greece in the 1980s. These years have no budget score.
    • 2026 is excluded: the year is incomplete.
